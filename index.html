The problem this exists to solve

James Fadiman has been thinking, speaking, writing, and teaching about psychedelics since 1961. He was a graduate student at Stanford when his former Harvard undergraduate adviser — Richard Alpert, who later became Ram Dass — introduced him to psilocybin in Paris. He guided Stewart Brand's first LSD trip in 1962. He participated in some of the last legal psychedelic research sessions before the government shut the field down. He was there at the beginning. He watched the entire field get buried. And he lived long enough to watch it come back.

The scoping page references Fadiman's 2011 and 2025 books and his 2019 paper with Sophia Korb; the 132-record catalogue itself covers recorded appearances. The paper describes an uncontrolled, self-selected reporting project involving more than 1,000 people in 59 countries. It does not establish efficacy, safety, or suitability for any person. Primary record: PubMed 30925850 ↗

The Atlas is intended to make connections across recorded appearances easier to explore, while keeping each record tied to its source and date.

The catalogue spans a 1968 KQED documentary through April 2026. Its historical status snapshot labels 50 records dead, while 15 records have no URL; two records meet both conditions. That produces 63 records to investigate, without establishing that the underlying recordings have been removed.

The preserved catalogue statuses come from the earlier 29 August 2026 snapshot and are not current availability guarantees. A dated recovery review on 24 October records which source IDs were checked and which remain unreviewed. The source-recovery section records candidate alternatives and the limits of each check. A catalogue match helps locate a source; it does not establish the accuracy of a statement.

What has been done — and what has been deliberately held

Current project status

The reproducible work in this repository is a 132-record catalogue, historical link-status inventory, local search and filters, timeline, annotation demonstration, and proposed schema and technical architecture. Ten additional-source candidates remain subject to identity and source review. Full-archive completeness has not been established.

Earlier scoping text described three mapped books and more than twenty inventoried academic papers. The selected bibliography now records three books and three papers with source links. It does not reproduce the earlier claim of more than twenty papers, and no full book or paper text has been ingested. Transcription and a production retrieval system are not running in this Atlas. The passage explorer can locally load user-supplied transcript JSON for passage search and chronological comparison; it does not mean this catalogue has been transcribed.

The step that has not been taken is ingestion: downloading the audio, running transcription, putting his words into a system. That step has been deliberately withheld. The material is his. Beginning without his awareness felt like the wrong place to start something designed to honor sixty-plus years of work.

The annotation schema — evidence-boundary specification

This proposed schema records what a source explicitly says. It must preserve the source date, speaker, quotation span, and review status. Public tooling does not translate historical terminology into dosing guidance, infer biology, or convert a topic mention into an indication.

Proposed technical architecture — eight layers

This is a design outline, not deployed infrastructure. Vendor selections, costs and performance assumptions require fresh evaluation. The current Atlas runs as a static catalogue with local search; it does not automatically download, transcribe or continuously ingest recordings.

Layer 1 — Source intake

Record stable source IDs, original URLs, acquisition dates and a clear basis for processing. Preserve original media metadata and checksums. Repeated intake should detect existing assets without overwriting their evidence history.

Layer 2 — Transcription

Import supplied transcripts or process appropriately sourced audio through a selected transcription service or local model. Preserve timestamps and speaker labels as provisional until checked against the recording. Do not identify a speaker solely from how long they speak.

Layer 3 — Passages

Divide transcripts into passages that retain source IDs, exact text spans, dates and available time offsets. Review repeated stories as related passages without silently deleting distinct appearances.

Layer 4 — Storage

Store versioned source, transcript and passage records separately. Keyword indexing can support initial discovery. A future vector index would need evaluation against known source passages before being described as verified semantic retrieval.

Layer 5 — Annotation

Keep source wording separate from proposed labels and interpretation. Source identity, date, quotation span, reviewer, limitations and review status should accompany annotations. Generated labels remain proposals until reviewed.

Layer 6 — Query interface

Return attributable passages with source IDs and available timestamps. The local passage explorer supports user-supplied transcript records; it ships without a transcribed Fadiman corpus. Catalogue matches alone cannot substantiate quotations or clinical conclusions.

Layer 7 — Tracing ideas over time

Compare dated passages and show where a topic appears in the supplied material. Differences in wording, audience or recording date do not automatically establish a change of position. Any claimed evolution requires source-level review.

Layer 8 — Deployment and operations

A production service would need durable storage, access controls, recovery procedures and monitored ingestion jobs. No daily transcription cron or external model inference endpoint is active in this prototype. These operational choices remain future implementation work.

What the cited research can and cannot show

Fadiman & Korb (2019). An uncontrolled, self-selected reporting project with more than 1,000 participants in 59 countries. It can describe reported experiences; it cannot determine efficacy, safety, causation, or individual suitability. PubMed 30925850 ↗

Bershad et al. (2019). A placebo-controlled low-dose LSD study in healthy volunteers with a narrow experimental scope. It is not treatment evidence and does not support personalized use. PubMed 31331617 ↗

Polito & Stevenson (2019). An observational study of self-reported microdosing. Observational associations do not establish therapeutic benefit or safety. PubMed 30726251 ↗

Scope rule. Atlas records and search results are historical discovery metadata. Health-related statements remain attributable to their original speakers and dates; they are not endorsed or converted into advice here.

Design principle — provenance before interpretation

Every statement the finished system produces should stay linked to its original recording, speaker, date, quotation span, and review status. The public prototype deliberately limits itself to catalog discovery and deterministic language flags.

Review principle — domain experts remain accountable

Health, scientific, legal, and safety claims require review by appropriately qualified independent professionals. The system must surface uncertainty and disagreement instead of generating a clinical conclusion.

Safety and limitations

This is an archival discovery tool, not medical guidance. It is not a dose calculator, contraindication screen, interaction checker, diagnosis tool, treatment tool, or emergency resource. It does not personalize output and must not be used to decide whether, when, or how to take, stop, combine, or change any substance or medication.

Catalog records and heuristic flags may be incomplete, outdated, or wrong. Follow source links, check publication dates, and seek appropriately licensed professional care for health decisions. In an emergency, contact local emergency services.

Where things stand and what comes next

This public build is a catalog, filterable explorer, timeline, deterministic term flagger, and local source-ID search prototype. It does not contain full transcripts, a production ingestion pipeline, verified semantic search, or a clinical knowledge system. The larger ingestion and review system remains unbuilt.

The step that has not been taken is ingestion: downloading the audio, running transcription, putting his words into the system. That step has been deliberately withheld. The material is his. Beginning without his awareness felt like the wrong place to start something designed to honor sixty-plus years of work.

To take this further — with his input on what he most wants it to do — the build is ready to begin. If the timing is not right, the scope work and infrastructure exist either way.
